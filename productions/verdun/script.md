# Script: Verdun: The Battle Designed to Bleed France White

16:9 · 113 lines · 1665 words

## Hook

1. In February 1916, more than twelve hundred German guns opened fire on a quiet French fortress town.
2. For ten months, two armies fed nearly three quarters of a million men into the same few miles.
3. Germany's top general had a plan that was not really about capturing ground at all.
4. This is the Battle of Verdun, the battle designed to bleed France white.
5. And this is how the trap he set swallowed his own army as well.

## Only France Remains

6. By the end of 1915, the Western Front had frozen into trenches from the Channel to Switzerland.
7. Millions of men faced each other across mud and barbed wire, and neither side could break through.
8. In Berlin, the German Chief of the General Staff, Erich von Falkenhayn, searched for another way.
9. He believed the war would be won or lost in France, not in Russia or Italy.
10. Britain, he thought, was the real enemy, and France was the sword in Britain's hand.
11. Break France, and Britain would have no ally on the continent strong enough to fight on.
12. His answer was not a breakthrough, but a place the French would defend at any cost.
13. That place was Verdun, an ancient fortress city on the River Meuse.
14. Verdun sat in a bulge in the French line, ringed by forts and pushing into German-held ground.
15. It had guarded France's eastern frontier for centuries, and losing it would shatter French morale.
16. In his memoirs, Falkenhayn claimed he had told the Kaiser he would bleed France to death.
17. Historians still argue whether that Christmas memorandum was real, or written after the war.
18. Whatever the truth, his plan relied on German artillery doing most of the killing.

## Operation Judgement

19. The Germans called the attack Operation Gericht, meaning Judgement, and prepared it in secret.
20. They massed 1,201 guns, two thirds of them heavy, against roughly six hundred French guns.
21. Trains carried mountains of shells to depots hidden in the forests north of the city.
22. The attack was set for February twelfth, but fog, rain and snow delayed it for nine days.
23. Then, at a quarter past seven on the morning of February 21, the guns opened fire.
24. For about ten hours, roughly a million shells fell on a front only thirty kilometres wide.
25. Woods were shredded into stumps, and French trenches simply vanished into the earth.
26. In the Bois des Caures, Lieutenant Colonel Émile Driant held on with two battalions of chasseurs.
27. Driant was killed on February 22, and only 118 of his men escaped from the wood.
28. In four days, the Germans pushed forward about four miles toward the city.
29. Ahead of them stood Fort Douaumont, the largest and strongest fort guarding Verdun.
30. Its layers of concrete and earth had been built to withstand the heaviest shells of the age.
31. Yet in 1915, the French had stripped most of its guns for use elsewhere on the front.
32. On February 25, about one hundred German soldiers walked into the fort almost unopposed.
33. Inside, they found a warrant officer and about twenty-five men, who surrendered without a fight.
34. In Germany, church bells rang, and newspapers hailed the capture as a great victory.
35. Across France, the loss was a humiliation, and it would take eight months to win the fort back.

## The Sacred Way

36. That same night, General Philippe Pétain took command of the defence of Verdun.
37. He was a careful infantry general who believed firepower, not courage alone, won battles.
38. His first order was simple: there would be no retreat.
39. Pétain's real problem was that Verdun had only one road and one narrow railway to the rear.
40. That road ran south to the railhead at Bar-le-Duc, and it became the city's lifeline.
41. The French gathered about 3,500 trucks and kept them moving day and night.
42. By March, one truck passed every fourteen seconds, carrying men, shells and food to the front.
43. Labour battalions shovelled stone onto the road constantly to stop it collapsing beneath them.
44. In its first month, the road delivered about 263,000 men to the battlefield.
45. The soldiers came to call it La Voie Sacrée, the Sacred Way.
46. Pétain also rotated divisions in and out quickly, a system the French called the noria.
47. Fresh troops marched up toward the guns while exhausted survivors trudged back down beside them.
48. By May, forty-two French divisions had already passed through the furnace of Verdun.
49. Over the year, about three quarters of the entire French Army fought there.
50. The burden spread across the whole nation, and almost every French family felt its weight.
51. On April 10, Pétain's order of the day ended with a famous line: Courage, we'll get them.

## The Mill on the Meuse

52. Unable to break through on the east bank, the Germans widened the attack across the river.
53. In March, they struck the west bank, where French guns had been raking their flank.
54. Two hills became killing grounds: Hill 304, and a ridge called Le Mort Homme, the Dead Man.
55. For weeks, the same slopes were taken, lost and retaken under endless bombardment.
56. On May 4, the Germans seized the northern slope of Hill 304, but the French held the crest.
57. Soldiers began to describe the battlefield as a mill that ground up everyone sent into it.
58. Men lived in flooded shell holes, often cut off from food and water for days.
59. Messages often travelled by runner or carrier pigeon, because telephone wires were cut almost at once.
60. In June, the fighting reached Fort Vaux, the next fortress on the road to Verdun.
61. Its commander, Major Sylvain-Eugène Raynal, had taken over the fort only weeks earlier.
62. On June 2, German troops climbed onto the fort's roof and attacked with flamethrowers.
63. The battle moved underground, where men fought from barricades in pitch-dark corridors.
64. Soon the water ran out, and some of what remained turned out to be poisoned.
65. Raynal released his last carrier pigeon, Le Vaillant, with a desperate plea for relief.
66. Badly gassed, the pigeon reached the citadel and died, later honoured with the Legion of Honour.
67. On June 7, out of water, food and ammunition, Raynal's garrison surrendered.
68. Crown Prince Wilhelm was so impressed that he presented Raynal with a French officer's sword.

## They Shall Not Pass

69. Falkenhayn's plan was failing on its own terms, because German losses were climbing almost as fast.
70. Every French counterattack had to be met by German infantry, who died in the same mud.
71. Still, the German command pressed on toward the last ridges in front of the city.
72. On June 22, German guns fired more than 116,000 shells filled with a new gas, diphosgene.
73. The Germans called it Green Cross, after the marking painted on each shell.
74. More than 1,600 French soldiers were struck down as the gas crept across their positions.
75. The next day, about 80,000 German infantry surged forward and captured the village of Fleury.
76. It was the closest the German army would ever come to taking Verdun.
77. That day, General Robert Nivelle issued an order to his men: You will not let them pass.
78. The phrase became They shall not pass, and it was later wrongly credited to Pétain.
79. Fleury changed hands sixteen times that summer, until almost nothing of the village remained.
80. On July 11, the Germans launched one last major attack against Fort Souville.
81. About thirty German soldiers reached the top of the fort, less than two miles from Verdun.
82. They were driven off, and the German offensive at Verdun had reached its limit.

## The Tide Turns

83. Far to the east, Russia's Brusilov Offensive, launched on June 4, smashed into Austria-Hungary.
84. Then, on July 1, British and French armies attacked along the River Somme.
85. Germany now had to pull guns and divisions away from Verdun to plug the gaps.
86. Falkenhayn ordered his armies at Verdun to stop attacking and hold what they had.
87. On August 29, the Kaiser replaced him with Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff.
88. Within days, the new commanders called off the German offensive at Verdun.
89. Now the French struck back, led by Nivelle and the aggressive General Charles Mangin.
90. They used a creeping barrage, a wall of shells moving just ahead of their advancing infantry.
91. On October 24, after a six-day bombardment, French troops recaptured Fort Douaumont.
92. In two days, they took more than 6,000 German prisoners.
93. On November 2, the Germans abandoned Fort Vaux, and it too returned to French hands.
94. A final French attack began on December 15, behind more than a million shells.
95. It captured over 11,000 prisoners and 115 guns, and the battle ended on December 18.
96. After ten months, the front line stood close to where it had been in February.

## The Price of Verdun

97. Verdun cost France around 377,000 casualties, and Germany around 337,000.
98. Together, that is roughly 714,000 men killed, wounded, missing or captured.
99. In the first five months alone, the two armies fired more than forty million shells.
100. Falkenhayn had meant to bleed France white, but Germany had bled almost as heavily.
101. Nine villages were wiped off the map, and six of them were never rebuilt.
102. Even today, each of those empty villages keeps an appointed mayor, caretaker of a place with no residents.
103. Near the old forts stands the Douaumont Ossuary, inaugurated in 1932.
104. It holds the bones of at least 130,000 unidentified French and German soldiers.
105. In front of it lie more than 16,000 graves, the largest French military cemetery of the war.
106. The battle created heroes, but some of its lessons would prove dangerous.
107. Nivelle's success won him command of the French Army, but his 1917 offensive ended in disaster and mutiny.
108. Pétain was called in to restore order, and France remembered him as the saviour of Verdun.
109. In 1940, that reputation carried him to power as head of the Vichy regime that collaborated with Germany.

## Conclusion

110. Verdun was meant to be a machine for killing Frenchmen while German guns did the work.
111. Instead, it became a mill that ground up both armies, for almost no ground at all.
112. For France, holding Verdun became proof that the nation could suffer almost anything and endure.
113. Falkenhayn wanted France to bleed white, but at Verdun, both nations bled into the same mud.
