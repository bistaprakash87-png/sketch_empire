# Script: How the Sinking of the Lusitania Turned America Against Germany

16:9 · Beau Whitaker · 114 lines


## Hook

1. On a calm spring afternoon in 1915, a single torpedo struck the most famous liner on the Atlantic.
2. Eighteen minutes later, she was gone, and nearly twelve hundred people had died with her.
3. One hundred and twenty-eight of the dead were citizens of a country not even at war.
4. Here's how the sinking of the Lusitania turned America against Germany.
5. And why it still took two more years before American soldiers went to war.

## A Neutral Nation

6. When war broke out in Europe in August 1914, the United States chose to stay out.
7. President Woodrow Wilson asked Americans to be neutral in thought as well as action.
8. Millions of Americans had roots in Germany, Ireland and Britain, and few wanted another country's war.
9. Yet American factories and banks were already trading heavily with Britain and France.
10. Britain's navy blockaded Germany, choking the flow of food and raw materials to its ports.
11. Germany answered with a new kind of weapon that struck from beneath the waves.

## The Warning

12. In February 1915, Germany declared the waters around Britain a war zone.
13. Enemy merchant ships in that zone could now be sunk without warning, and neutrals sailed at risk.
14. Wilson replied that Germany would be held to strict accountability for any American lives lost.
15. In March, an American named Leon Thrasher drowned when a U-boat sank the British ship Falaba.
16. Then, on May 1, a U-boat torpedoed the American tanker Gulflight, and three men died.
17. At the centre of this danger sailed the Lusitania, pride of the Cunard line.
18. When she entered service in 1907, she was one of the largest and fastest ships ever built.
18a. In 1907 she won the Blue Riband for the fastest Atlantic crossing, taking the title from Germany. [F-added: Wikipedia RMS Lusitania]
19. Her passengers included millionaires, families, nannies and children, all bound for Liverpool.
20. On the morning she sailed, a strange notice appeared in American newspapers.
21. The German embassy warned that travellers on British ships sailed at their own risk.
22. In some papers, it was printed right beside Cunard's advertisement for the Lusitania.
23. Almost nobody cancelled, because no one believed Germany would sink a ship full of civilians.
24. On May 1, 1915, the Lusitania left New York with nearly two thousand people aboard.

## Eighteen Minutes

25. Commanding her was Captain William Turner, one of Cunard's most experienced captains.
26. To save coal, one of her four boiler rooms had been shut down, cutting her top speed.
27. Meanwhile, off the south coast of Ireland, a German submarine was hunting.
28. The liner was just eleven miles off the Irish coast, almost within sight of safety.
29. U-20 was commanded by Walther Schwieger, a young officer who had already sunk several ships that week.
30. On May 7, Turner slowed his ship in thick fog as he neared the Old Head of Kinsale.
31. Through his periscope, Schwieger saw four funnels rising above the horizon.
32. At ten minutes past two in the afternoon, he fired a single torpedo.
33. It struck the starboard side, just behind the bridge.
34. Seconds later, a second, much larger explosion tore through the ship from inside.
35. In his war diary, Schwieger noted an unusually heavy explosion and a huge cloud of smoke.
36. Historians still argue about that second blast, from boilers and steam to coal dust or cargo.
37. The Lusitania lurched hard to starboard, and water poured into her open portholes.
38. Her steep tilt made most lifeboats impossible to lower safely.
39. Of her forty-eight lifeboats, only six were successfully lowered.
40. Passengers jumped into the cold water, clinging to deck chairs, oars and wreckage.
41. Just eighteen minutes after the torpedo struck, the great ship slipped beneath the sea.
42. Schwieger did not stay to help, and U-20 slipped away toward home.
43. Fishing boats and steamers rushed from the coast to pull survivors from the water.
44. Survivors and the dead were brought to the Irish port of Queenstown.
45. Nearly twelve hundred people were lost, including ninety-four children.
45a. Only around seven hundred and sixty passengers and crew survived. [Wikipedia: ~764 survivors]

## America's Fury

46. Within hours, the news flashed across the Atlantic by telegraph.
47. American newspapers filled their front pages with the names of the dead.
48. Among them were famous Americans, like the millionaire Alfred Vanderbilt and the producer Charles Frohman.
49. Of the one hundred and fifty-nine Americans aboard, one hundred and twenty-eight never came home.
50. Crowds gathered outside newspaper offices in New York, waiting for each new list of names.
51. Former president Theodore Roosevelt called the attack piracy and demanded action.
52. Editorials called it murder on the high seas and a crime against civilisation.
53. Germany insisted that the Lusitania was a legitimate target carrying weapons for Britain.
54. Berlin even claimed the ship was armed, but American inspectors had found no guns aboard.
55. Her cargo did include millions of rifle cartridges and over a thousand cases of shrapnel shells.
55a. That cargo was listed openly on the ship’s manifest when she left New York. [Wikipedia: manifest listed ~4.2M cartridges]
56. But to most Americans, that could never justify drowning women and children without warning.
57. For the first time, many ordinary Americans saw Germany not as a distant power, but as a threat.

## Wilson's Tightrope

58. Woodrow Wilson now faced the hardest choice of his presidency.
59. Three days later, he told a Philadelphia audience that a nation could be too proud to fight.
60. The phrase was mocked by critics, who saw it as weakness in the face of murder.
61. Instead of war, Wilson sent Germany a series of sharp diplomatic notes.
62. The first, in May, demanded that Germany stop attacking unarmed passenger ships.
63. His Secretary of State, William Jennings Bryan, feared the notes would drag America into war.
64. America's ambassador in London warned that the country must declare war or lose Europe's respect.
65. On June 9, rather than sign the second note, Bryan resigned.
66. His replacement, Robert Lansing, was far more sympathetic to Britain.
67. A third note in July warned that further attacks would be seen as deliberately unfriendly.
68. Germany's replies defended the sinking and refused to admit it had been illegal.
69. Wilson was walking a tightrope between public anger and his promise to keep America at peace.

## The Propaganda War

70. Britain understood the power of the tragedy, and quickly turned it into a weapon.
71. In Germany, an artist named Karl Goetz made a satirical medal mocking the sinking.
71a. It was dated May the fifth, two days early, and Britain called that error proof of a plan. [IWM]
72. British officials made huge numbers of copies and sold them as proof of German cruelty.
72a. Around three hundred thousand copies were sold, raising money for the Red Cross and blinded soldiers. [IWM: ~300,000; St Dunstan’s and Red Cross]
73. Days after the sinking, Britain also published the Bryce Report on German atrocities in Belgium.
74. Many of its stories were later shown to be exaggerated, but in 1915 they were widely believed.
75. In America, a famous recruiting poster showed a drowning mother clutching her baby beneath one word: Enlist.
76. The Lusitania became a symbol of a war being fought not just by armies, but against civilians.
77. Anger over the sinking also boosted a Preparedness Movement demanding a bigger American army.
78. That summer, volunteers began training as officers at a camp in Plattsburgh, New York.

## Germany Backs Down

79. In Berlin, German leaders realised they could not afford to push America any further.
80. Germany's admirals were furious, convinced their submarines could win the war if they were unleashed.
81. But in August 1915, another U-boat sank the British liner Arabic, and more Americans died.
82. Facing a break with Washington, Germany promised not to sink passenger liners without warning.
82a. The German ambassador, Count Johann von Bernstorff, delivered this Arabic pledge in September 1915. [Wikipedia SS Arabic: 1 Sept 1915]
83. In March 1916, a torpedo hit the French ferry Sussex, injuring several Americans aboard.
83a. Around eighty people died, among them the Spanish composer Enrique Granados. [Wikipedia Sussex pledge]
84. Wilson threatened to cut diplomatic ties, and Germany promised again to restrain its submarines.
85. For the rest of 1916, the U-boats held back, and America stayed out of the war.
86. In 1916, Wilson won re-election with a simple slogan: He kept us out of war.
87. Yet beneath that peace, American trade, loans and sympathy were flowing steadily toward Britain.

## The Road to War

88. By early 1917, Germany's generals believed they could starve Britain before America could act.
89. On February 1, Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare across the Atlantic.
90. Germany knew this would probably bring America in, and gambled that it would not matter.
91. Two days later, Wilson broke off diplomatic relations with Germany.
92. Then British codebreakers revealed a secret German message called the Zimmermann Telegram.
93. It offered Mexico Texas, New Mexico and Arizona if it joined a war against the United States.
93a. Britain had decoded it in January, and handed it to Washington on February 24. [National Archives]
94. When the telegram was published on March 1, Americans were outraged.
95. In March, U-boats sank several American merchant ships, and public patience finally ran out.
96. On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked Congress for a declaration of war against Germany.
97. He told them the world must be made safe for democracy.
98. Four days later, on April 6, Congress voted for war.
98a. The Senate voted eighty-two to six, and the House three hundred seventy-three to fifty. [Miller Center]
99. As American soldiers sailed for France, the memory of the Lusitania sailed with them.

## Conclusion

100. The Lusitania did not take America to war on its own.
101. That took two years, broken promises, a secret telegram and more ships at the bottom of the sea.
102. But it was the moment millions of Americans first saw Germany as an enemy.
103. In 1925, Germany finally paid compensation to the families of American victims.
104. Today, the wreck still lies on the seabed off the Irish coast, a hundred metres below the waves.
105. The Lusitania did not drag America into the war, but it decided which side America would fight on.
